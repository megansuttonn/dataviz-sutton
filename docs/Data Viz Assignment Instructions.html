Data Viz

Data Visualization Assignment: Telling a Story with Data

Purpose

The goal of this assignment is not simply to make several good-looking graphs. Your goal is to use a sequence of visualizations to communicate an empirical story: to guide a reader through a question, pattern, comparison, complication, or resolution in a deliberate order.

A useful test: if your figures could be rearranged in any order without changing the meaning of the presentation, you probably have a collection of plots rather than a data story.

You will then tell the same underlying story twice. The evidence should remain the same, but your design choices, level of technical detail, prose, annotations, and use of interactivity should reflect two different communication settings:

  1. A scientific article
  2. An accessible/dynamic web-based outlet

At least two substantive data visualizations are required in each version.

Learning Goals

By completing this assignment, you should be able to:

  • Identify a clear empirical message in a dataset and organize evidence into a coherent narrative arc.
  • Create multiple visualizations that perform distinct jobs within that narrative rather than redundantly displaying the same result.
  • Use R to produce polished, reproducible data visualizations and a rendered web document.
  • Adapt visualization design and explanatory text to different audiences and publication contexts.
  • Distinguish scientific precision and technical reporting from public-facing clarity, accessibility, and engagement.
  • Use Git and GitHub to make the complete analysis reproducible and GitHub Pages to publish the finished work.

1. Define the Story

Begin with the story, not the chart type.

Before building your final visualizations, decide what you want a reader to learn and how the evidence needs to unfold.

Story Summary — 250 Words Maximum

At the beginning of your site, provide a concise summary of the story you are trying to tell.

This is not an abstract describing everything you did. Instead, identify:

  • the central question, observation, or puzzle;
  • the key development, comparison, or tension; and
  • the conclusion, resolution, or takeaway that the visual evidence supports.

Use Claus Wilke’s discussion of storytelling as a guide:

Fundamentals of Data Visualization — Chapter 29: Telling a Story and Making a Point
https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/telling-a-story.html

In particular, attend to the idea that figures should have a clear purpose within a story arc.

2. Tell the Story with Visualizations

After your summary, walk the reader through the story using text and at least two substantive data visualizations.

The prose and figures should work together. Introduce what the reader needs to know, direct their attention toward the relevant evidence, and explain why each new visualization advances the story.

Your visualizations should satisfy the following principles:

  • Each visualization should have a clear purpose in the sequence.

  • The sequence should create progression. For example:

    establish a pattern → introduce a comparison or complication → resolve or reinterpret the initial pattern

  • Use informative titles, labels, captions, and/or annotations so readers know what they should notice.

  • Do not include a plot merely because you made it during data exploration. Every final figure should earn its place in the story.

  • At least two visualizations are required. Use more when they genuinely improve the narrative.

3. Tell the Same Story for Two Outlets

You will now tell the same empirical story twice.

The underlying evidence and substantive conclusions should remain consistent. What should change is how you communicate that evidence to the intended audience.

Version A: Scientific Article

Present your story as though the figures and accompanying text were being prepared for a conventional scientific journal article.

Prioritize precision, interpretability, reproducibility, and technical completeness.

Your scientific version should generally:

  • Use static figures.
  • Be fully interpretable in black-and-white or greyscale as is often required by scientific journals. Do not rely on colour to encode essential information.
  • Use conventional scientific labels, units, scales, legends/direct labels, and figure captions as appropriate.
  • Report technical information needed to interpret the visualization—for example, sample sizes, uncertainty, model quantities, or definitions where relevant.
  • Keep decorative elements restrained. The visual hierarchy should emphasize the data and the scientific point.
  • Use prose appropriate for a scientifically literate reader. Technical terminology is appropriate when it improves precision.

Version B: Accessible / Dynamic Outlet

Now present the same empirical story for an audience encountering it through an engaging web article, explainer, or similar public-facing medium.

Prioritize clarity, accessibility, narrative flow, and reader engagement without changing or overstating the evidence.

Your accessible/dynamic version might:

  • Use plain-language, takeaway-oriented headings and explanatory text.
  • Use colour, highlighting, direct annotation, icons, small multiples, or other design devices when they help guide attention.
  • Use interactive or dynamic elements—for example, tooltips, filters, animation, or plotly—when they add genuine explanatory value. Interactivity is cool, but is not required for its own sake.
  • Reduce unnecessary technical detail in the main visual display while preserving enough context for the claims to be understood accurately.
  • Make deliberate accessibility choices: readable text, sufficient contrast, sensible colour use, clear labels, and alternatives to colour-only encoding.
  • Think about pacing. Guide the reader through the evidence rather than confronting them with all available information at once.

Important: Adaptation Is Not Decoration

The two versions should not simply be identical figures with a different ggplot2 theme applied.

Your choices should demonstrate that you understand how audience and medium change what information is foregrounded, how it is explained, and how the reader’s attention is directed, while the underlying empirical claims remain consistent.

Exemplars: What to Pay Attention To

The following examples illustrate different aspects of effective visual storytelling.

1. Good Authority: Republicans’ Fascination with Testosterone, in Three Charts

https://goodauthority.org/news/republicans-fascination-with-testosterone-in-three-charts/

This is close to the scope of the story expected for this assignment.

Pay particular attention to how three relatively conventional plots become an argument through sequencing. Each visualization answers or develops something raised by the previous one, while the prose explains why the next piece of evidence matters.

Notice that the effectiveness of the piece does not depend on technically elaborate visualizations. The strength comes from deciding:

What should the reader see first? What question does that create? What evidence should they see next?

2. The Economist: The Banana Index

Data and materials:
https://github.com/TheEconomist/banana-index-data

This example illustrates how repeating a visual grammar while changing the analytical perspective can itself tell a story.

The comparison changes depending on how the environmental impact of food is evaluated—for example, relative to weight, calories, or protein.

Pay attention to how holding much of the visual form constant makes it easier for the reader to focus on the thing that is changing: the basis of comparison.

This is also an excellent example of making a potentially technical measurement issue intuitive and visually accessible.

3. The Pudding: Is the Love Song Dying?

https://pudding.cool/2024/11/love-songs/

Consider this an aspirational example of public-facing data storytelling.

You are not expected to reproduce the sophistication of its web development.

Instead, pay attention to:

  • pacing;
  • progressive disclosure;
  • annotation;
  • interactivity where it is relevant and useful;
  • accessible language;
  • transitions between pieces of evidence; and
  • how successive visualizations set up, qualify, or reinterpret the initial claim.

Think about when the authors choose to show each piece of information rather than merely what visualization technique they use.

Learning from the Exemplars

Do not imitate these examples mechanically.

Instead, use them to study how authors decide:

What does the reader see first?

What question or tension does that create?

What evidence comes next?

How does that evidence change what the reader thinks about the previous visualization?

What should the reader conclude by the end?

That sequence—not simply the number or attractiveness of the plots—is what turns several visualizations into a data story.

Required Website Structure

Your GitHub Pages site should make the following four components easy to find.

1. Story Summary

250 words or fewer.

State the empirical story you intend to tell.

2. Version A: Scientific Article

Text plus at least two visualizations that tell your story in a scientific-journal style.

3. Version B: Accessible / Dynamic Outlet

Text plus at least two visualizations that tell the same story for a broader, web-oriented audience.

4. Data / Source Note

Identify the dataset and its source. Briefly describe any important limitations, transformations, or other information needed to understand your analysis.

Reproducibility and GitHub Requirements

Your repository should contain everything needed to understand and reproduce the rendered site.

A reader should not have to reconstruct your workflow from copied-and-pasted output.

Your repository should include:

  • All R/Quarto source files used to generate the site.
  • The data, when redistribution is permitted, or clear code/instructions for obtaining the data when it is not.
  • All data cleaning, transformation, analysis, and visualization code required for the final output.
  • A sensible project structure and informative file names.
  • A rendered GitHub Pages site that works from the submitted URL.
  • No broken figures, missing local files, or code that depends on unexplained objects from your interactive R session.

What to Submit

Submit exactly two links through the course submission system:

  1. GitHub repository URL — the repository containing your complete source code/project.
  2. GitHub Pages URL — the live website where the finished assignment is rendered and served.

Before submitting, open both links in a private/incognito browser window and confirm that they are accessible and that the site renders correctly.

What Strong Work Will Demonstrate

Story

A focused empirical point with a discernible narrative arc. The figures collectively move the reader from one idea to the next.

Purposeful Visualization

Each figure makes a clear point, uses an appropriate visual encoding, and contributes something distinct to the story.

Audience Adaptation

The scientific and accessible/dynamic versions make substantively different and well-justified communication choices, rather than superficial changes in themes or colours.

Clarity and Accessibility

Titles, annotations, labels, scales, colour/greyscale choices, and prose make the intended interpretation easy to follow without misleading the reader.

Technical Quality

Visualizations are accurate, polished, and appropriate to the data. Code is functional and reproducible.

Reproducible Publication

The repository is organized and complete, and the GitHub Pages site renders successfully from the submitted materials.

Final Check Before You Submit

Ask yourself:

  • Can I state my story in a few sentences without simply listing the chart types I made?
  • Do I have at least two substantive visualizations in each version?
  • Does every figure have a specific job in the story?
  • Would changing the order of my figures weaken or change the narrative? If not, have I really created a sequence?
  • Does the scientific version look and read like material that could plausibly appear in a scientific article?
  • Does the accessible/dynamic version guide a broader reader toward the same evidence without sacrificing accuracy?
  • Are both versions telling the same underlying empirical story?
  • Can someone reproduce the site from my repository?
  • Do both submitted URLs work?
